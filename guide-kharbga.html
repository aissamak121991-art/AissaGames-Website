How to play Kharbga

The rules, the strategy and the vocabulary of the traditional North African board game.

In short Kharbga is a two-player strategy game on a 7×7 board. Each player has 24 pieces. You first place your pieces, then move them one step at a time, capturing by trapping an enemy piece between two of yours. Whoever has no pieces left loses.

1. About Kharbga

Kharbga (الخربقة) is a traditional board game of North Africa. It is played with very simple equipment: a grid and two kinds of small pieces, such as stones and date pits. There are no dice and no luck. Every outcome comes from observation, planning and patience, which is why the game is often compared with draughts and other classic abstract games.

Our digital edition keeps the rules intact. It does not highlight legal moves or give hints during a match, so you learn to read the board the way players do at a real table.

2. The board and the pieces

  • The board is a 7×7 grid of 49 squares, called houses (dar).
  • Each player owns 24 pieces, called dogs (kalb), 48 in total.
  • The centre square is special only during placement: it must stay empty. Once the battle starts, it is an ordinary square.

Because 48 pieces fill 48 of the 49 squares, the board is almost completely full when the battle begins. Only the centre is free, so the first moves are very restricted, and this is exactly what makes the opening so tactical.

3. Phase 1: Placement

The match starts with an empty board. Players take turns placing pieces, and on each turn you place exactly two pieces on any empty square except the centre. The first player starts. The phase ends when all 48 pieces are on the board, leaving only the centre empty.

Placement is not just setup, it is the first half of the game. Where you put your pieces decides which of them can move later and which will be stuck. Good players build solid groups, keep access to the squares next to the centre, and avoid leaving single pieces that can be trapped as soon as the battle starts.

4. Phase 2: Battle

The player who started the placement also moves first in the battle. On your turn you normally move one piece exactly one square, up, down, left or right, into an empty square. These moves are forbidden:

  • diagonal moves,
  • jumping over other pieces,
  • moving more than one square.

At the start only the four squares touching the centre can be entered, so the pieces beside the centre are the first to move. Every move you make opens a square for someone else, so each step has to be thought through.

5. How captures work

You capture by sandwiching. If an enemy piece is between two of your pieces in a straight line, horizontally or vertically, after your move, it is removed from the board. Diagonal sandwiches do not count.

A single move can capture several enemy pieces at once, in different directions. All of them are removed together. Capturing is optional: you may ignore an available capture and make another move, for example to set a trap.

6. Capture chains and keeping the turn

When your move captures at least one piece, you keep the turn. You may then move any of your pieces, the one that just captured or a different one. The turn passes to your opponent only after a move that does not capture. A well-prepared position can therefore win many pieces in a single turn, and a careless move can give one away.

7. Frozen pieces and locks

A piece that has no legal move is frozen. It stays on the board and is not removed, and it can move again later if a neighbouring square opens. Frozen does not mean dead.

If a player has no legal move at all, that player is locked. Locking is a tactic, not a victory: it costs the locked player their turn but does not end the match. While the opponent is locked, you keep playing, and the lock ends when you give the opponent something legal to do, such as an empty square to step into. A piece never leaves the board except by being captured. To keep matches from stalling, the app limits how many moves in a row can keep an opponent locked.

8. How to win

  • Eliminate every enemy piece, or
  • your opponent resigns.

Being locked does not lose the game, and neither does having fewer pieces. Games are often turned around by a single well-timed chain.

9. Strategy tips for beginners

  1. Place in groups. Two pieces side by side are much harder to sandwich than isolated ones.
  2. Think about the centre. The squares next to the centre decide who can move first. Make sure you have a protected piece there.
  3. Check both directions. Before every move, look at the squares on both sides of each of your pieces, horizontally and vertically.
  4. Count what your move opens. Moving a piece leaves an empty square. Ask whether your opponent can use that square to capture.
  5. Do not rush to capture. A capture that leaves your piece exposed may cost you more than it wins.

10. Ideas for stronger players

  • Bait and punish. Because capturing is optional, you can offer a piece so that the capture pulls an enemy piece out of position, then take two in return.
  • Prepare the chain. Arrange pieces so that one capture creates the next sandwich. Since you keep the turn, the whole sequence is played before your opponent can react.
  • Use freezing. Pieces boxed in by their own side are useless. Keep your own pieces mobile while limiting the squares your opponent can reach.
  • Plan the lock. Locking an opponent gives you free moves in a row. Use them to rebuild your structure, not to wait.

11. Glossary

TermMeaning
Dar (house)One square of the 7×7 board.
Kalb (dog)A playing piece.
PlacementThe opening phase in which players place two pieces per turn.
BattleThe phase in which pieces move one square at a time.
SandwichTrapping an enemy piece between two of yours in a straight line.
FrozenA piece with no legal move; it stays on the board.
LockA position in which a player has no legal move at all; it costs their turn, not the match.
